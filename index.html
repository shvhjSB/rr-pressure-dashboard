What counts as pressure

For every ball we estimate how much the next delivery could swing either side’s chance of winning. That leverage folds in required rate, wickets, balls left, how close the game is and team strength. 1.0 is a typical ball.

How the rating is built

Win probability. A ball-by-ball model in the style of the WASP broadcast model: outcome probabilities by over, wickets in hand and required rate for four scoring eras, solved exactly by working back from the end of the chase, with team strength (Elo) added. Each match is scored by a version of the model that never saw it.

Pressure. Leverage is the expected size of the next ball’s swing in win probability, divided by the average. Pressure balls are the top quarter of leverage within each innings, so the 19th over when setting a total counts as well as the end of a chase.

Performance. Every ball is compared with what an average player would do in the same spot (over, wickets, required rate, phase, pace or spin, opposition strength, and for bowlers the batting position faced). Each component is pulled toward the average in proportion to its noise, so short records are trusted less. Recent seasons count more (each year back ×0.75), and T20Is against weak sides count less.

Eligibility. At least 120 pressure balls faced or bowled, at least 8 pressure innings or spells, and active in 2025 or 2026.

What the data can’t tell you. Raw pressure numbers repeat poorly from one set of matches to the next, so ranks beyond the top few are close calls. The model sees what happened on the field, not injuries, availability or matchups.
